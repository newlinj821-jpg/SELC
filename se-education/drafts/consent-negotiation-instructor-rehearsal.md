# SE Education — Consent and Negotiation (Instructor draft v0.1)
**90 minutes · discussion, roleplay without touch · requires educator review before publication**

## Outcomes
Learners will be able to (1) describe informed, specific, ongoing and freely given consent; (2) create a written agreement for a hypothetical activity; (3) distinguish boundaries, preferences and risk disclosures; (4) practice stop/pause language; (5) respond to coercion and unclear capacity; and (6) name a clear post-event follow-up step.

## Setup (before opening doors)
Provide 2 scenario cards per participant, pens and blank negotiation worksheet. Arrange chairs so participants can work individually without being visible to peers. Display opt-out alternatives. Ask co-facilitator to manage anonymous questions. Explain reporting resources and prohibit recording. No actual touch or physical play in this class.

## Instructor run of show
**00:00–00:08 | Opening and psychological safety**
Say: “Consent is not a phrase you say once before something starts. It is an ongoing agreement people make while they remain able to make a choice. Tonight we will practice words and decisions. We do not need anyone to disclose personal stories, and nobody needs to pair with a stranger.”
Do: Point to exits, accommodations, restrooms, content notice and anonymous question basket. Explain participation options: speaking, writing, observing or stepping out.
Check: “Can I refuse an exercise without telling you why?” Correct: yes.

**00:08–00:23 | Permission and capacity**
Say: “Permission needs information. It must be possible to say no without punishment or pressure. An agreed activity does not imply permission for another. Consent is not valid when someone cannot meaningfully understand or choose, such as due to severe impairment or unconsciousness.”
Do: Compare consent to three cards: “Yes, but not with an implement”; “Maybe, I don't know enough”; “Yes, until I ask to stop.” Ask students what information would make each situation clearer.
Explain: authority roles, prior relationship arrangements and a signed form do not override withdrawn consent.

**00:23–00:38 | Negotiation as a repeatable method**
Write six prompts on board: Who? What precisely? Where? When/how long? What risks and boundaries? How do we pause, stop and follow up?
Say: “Specificity reduces the burden of mind reading. You can negotiate an experience without promising that it will happen. Negotiation itself can end with ‘not today.’”
Do: Offer hypothetical example: “Two people consider a blindfolded listening exercise seated in chairs; no contact, five minutes maximum.” Invite students to identify missing information—noise level, ability to remove blindfold, potential anxiety, signal for pause, safe exit.
Demonstrate: “Would you be interested in X?” → “Only under Y.” → “I can agree to Y. Shall we include Z for safety?” → “I want to stop if W occurs.” → “We can also choose not to proceed.”

**00:38–00:48 | Red, yellow, and alternatives**
Say: “Words are tools, not magic. Everyone in a scene must know what they mean. If someone says stop or their behavior suggests distress, pause and assess even if you expected a special word. Nonverbal signals must be agreed beforehand and may fail.”
Do: Practice three vocal responses in a purely verbal roleplay: “Pause.” “Stop now.” “I changed my mind.” The other person practices: “Stopping. Do you need distance, assistance, or quiet?”
Discuss: Hearing or speech differences and practical alternatives; interruption is always allowed.

**00:48–00:58 | Break** 
Display resources and allow silent questions.

**00:58–01:12 | Coercion and difficult examples**
Scenario A: Person declines an activity and partner responds, “If you cared about me you would.” Ask which part is coercive and how to respond.
Scenario B: Someone agreed to a scene but becomes frozen and silent. Expected response: stop, check responsiveness and safety; do not infer continued agreement.
Scenario C: Someone consents to a particular implement but educator introduces another without asking. Expected: obtain separate permission before any change.
Scenario D: A participant is substantially impaired. Expected: do not initiate the activity.
Say: “Not all ambiguity is a violation, but ambiguity is a reason to stop and clarify, never a reason to push through.”

**01:12–01:23 | Independent practice**
Hand out blank worksheet: Proposal / Explicitly excluded / Physical and emotional considerations / Signals / Timing / Aftercare / Follow-up. Ask participants to complete one hypothetical scenario; do not ask them to reveal real limits.
Optional pair work: Read only the hypothetical proposal; partner asks clarifying questions. Switch roles. Alternative: complete worksheet alone.
Educator feedback: look for concrete wording, no implied blanket consent, a clear stop condition and a follow-up step.

**01:23–01:28 | Quiz and answer review**
Use the six questions below. Explain every missed item without judgment; do not suggest a quiz grants technical or clinical qualifications.

**01:28–01:30 | Closing**
Say: “Negotiation succeeds when everyone can understand the proposal and say yes, no, or not yet without consequence. You can change your mind. You should never need to prove your limits to have them respected. Thank you for practicing.”
Collect anonymous event feedback and give attendees the NCSF reference link.

## Quiz — private answer key
1. Consent remains valid when a participant changes their mind mid-activity. **False.** Prior agreement does not override revocation.
2. A person hesitates and says ‘maybe.’ Best next step? **Clarify without pressure; do not proceed until clear permission.**
3. Does an established D/s relationship make future practices automatically permitted? **No.**
4. A participant freezes and stops responding during a scene. **Stop and assess safety and capacity.**
5. Which written plan is clearest? **A named activity, boundaries, duration, risks, stop signal, and follow-up.**
6. The top introduces a new tool not previously discussed. **Pause and negotiate the change before using it.**
Passing rubric: 5/6 plus educator review of any incorrect questions involving withdrawal, impairment or distress.

## Facilitator intervention cards
- “But they said yes earlier.” Response: “Past consent explains the plan, not the present. Check current agreement.”
- “Is a safeword mandatory?” Response: “Clear ways to interrupt and stop are mandatory; standardized vocabulary is not. Ordinary ‘stop’ must be respected too.”
- “Can I tell someone's story as an example?” Response: “Please use hypothetical examples that protect identities; do not identify other participants.”
- “A participant reports a concern about another attendee.” Response: “Offer a private channel to staff and established incident procedures; do not investigate in a group discussion.”

## Accessibility
Provide printed large-text worksheets and digital accessible copies. Allow solo exercises. Do not require looking someone in the eye, speaking aloud, pairing up, physical roleplay, or specific pronouns. Provide a route for confidential accommodation requests.
