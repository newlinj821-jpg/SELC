# Sensory Play & Communication

**SE Education · Complete instructor rehearsal draft v1 · NOT APPROVED / NOT PUBLISHED**

- Course ID: `sensory-play`
- Level: Beginner
- Duration: 90 minutes; suggested maximum 16 learners
- Delivery: Nonsexual material exploration on inert targets; consent-led demonstration
- Educator qualification: Educator experienced with sensory techniques and accessibility
- Student access: only approved, confirmed enrollment in linked Workshop event; teacher plan never exposed to learners

## Learner outcomes
1. Describe different types of sensory input and variable sensitivity.
2. Discuss how deprivation affects communication and consent.
3. Plan an accessible, low-risk sensory activity.
4. Identify distress, allergy, temperature and environmental risk factors.
5. Negotiate an immediate stop signal and nonverbal alternatives.

## Instructor preparation
Use SE's current published rules, verify the event room and staff coverage, identify emergency contacts and quiet space, confirm an accessible way to participate without disclosure or touch, and obtain educator/SELC approval. Hold all participant data confidentially and avoid identifiable anecdotes. Prepare an anonymized question route and ensure demonstrations remain within the announced scope.

**Bring:**
- Clean fabric, feathers, smooth objects and sample blindfolds for display
- Temperature-neutral sample materials; no heated wax or ice on skin
- Allergy and cleaning information
- Accessible alternatives to blindfolding

## Verbatim facilitator run-of-show

### 00:00–00:10 · Open and establish boundaries (10 minutes)

**Say:** “Sensation differs among people; novelty should not mean surprise touching. We will experiment with objects, not with strangers' bodies.”

**Do / demonstrate:** Point out opt-outs and cleaning rules.

**Prompt / check:** Ask: is curiosity the same as consent?

**If someone declines:** Thank them, offer silent observation or private writing, and continue without calling attention to the choice. **If a safety issue arises:** Stop the exercise, secure the situation, summon appropriate venue assistance, and avoid improvising medical interventions beyond training.

### 00:10–00:25 · Sensory modalities (15 minutes)

**Say:** “Texture, sound, visual conditions and expectation all influence perception. Preferences are personal and may fluctuate.”

**Do / demonstrate:** Present samples on a table; let participants observe rather than touch.

**Prompt / check:** Match sensory types to preferences hypothetically.

**If someone declines:** Thank them, offer silent observation or private writing, and continue without calling attention to the choice. **If a safety issue arises:** Stop the exercise, secure the situation, summon appropriate venue assistance, and avoid improvising medical interventions beyond training.

### 00:25–00:40 · Negotiation and triggers (15 minutes)

**Say:** “Ask about allergies, sensitivities, medication effects, startle response, mobility and restricted communication.”

**Do / demonstrate:** Facilitator models planning a sound-and-texture exercise.

**Prompt / check:** Write a no-touch hypothetical plan.

**If someone declines:** Thank them, offer silent observation or private writing, and continue without calling attention to the choice. **If a safety issue arises:** Stop the exercise, secure the situation, summon appropriate venue assistance, and avoid improvising medical interventions beyond training.

### 00:40–00:55 · Accessibility and alternatives (15 minutes)

**Say:** “Removing sight is not appropriate or welcome for everyone. Adapt the exercise to people with vision, hearing or sensory differences.”

**Do / demonstrate:** Demonstrate how to offer seated, self-guided, no-blindfold options.

**Prompt / check:** Learners rewrite one activity for different needs.

**If someone declines:** Thank them, offer silent observation or private writing, and continue without calling attention to the choice. **If a safety issue arises:** Stop the exercise, secure the situation, summon appropriate venue assistance, and avoid improvising medical interventions beyond training.

### 00:55–01:05 · Break (10 minutes)

**Say:** “Reset and clean samples.”

**Do / demonstrate:** Collect anonymous questions.

**Prompt / check:** Check sensory comfort.

**If someone declines:** Thank them, offer silent observation or private writing, and continue without calling attention to the choice. **If a safety issue arises:** Stop the exercise, secure the situation, summon appropriate venue assistance, and avoid improvising medical interventions beyond training.

### 01:05–01:18 · Educator demonstration (13 minutes)

**Say:** “A demonstration should show ongoing permission, calm pacing and an immediate stop option.”

**Do / demonstrate:** Instructor demonstrates tactile variation on inert materials with narration; no surprise participation.

**Prompt / check:** Students mark every explicit check-in.

**If someone declines:** Thank them, offer silent observation or private writing, and continue without calling attention to the choice. **If a safety issue arises:** Stop the exercise, secure the situation, summon appropriate venue assistance, and avoid improvising medical interventions beyond training.

### 01:18–01:26 · Troubleshooting (8 minutes)

**Say:** “Unanticipated distress, allergy concerns, discomfort or confusion are reasons to pause.”

**Do / demonstrate:** Review hypothetical overstimulation, lost nonverbal signal and unpleasant surprise.

**Prompt / check:** Write a stop-and-recover response.

**If someone declines:** Thank them, offer silent observation or private writing, and continue without calling attention to the choice. **If a safety issue arises:** Stop the exercise, secure the situation, summon appropriate venue assistance, and avoid improvising medical interventions beyond training.

### 01:26–01:30 · Quiz and close (4 minutes)

**Say:** “Better sensory play comes from curiosity paired with clear consent and responsiveness.”

**Do / demonstrate:** Quiz and resource handout.

**Prompt / check:** Ask what accessibility choice made learning easier.

**If someone declines:** Thank them, offer silent observation or private writing, and continue without calling attention to the choice. **If a safety issue arises:** Stop the exercise, secure the situation, summon appropriate venue assistance, and avoid improvising medical interventions beyond training.

## Instructor scenarios and contingency responses

- **“Can we skip negotiation if we know each other?”** Say: “Familiarity may help communication, but does not replace current permission. We will practice clear questions regardless.”
- **“Can I just watch?”** Say: “Yes. Watching is a valid way to participate, and you can decline discussion, demonstration or practice.”
- **A participant asks about a technique beyond this class.** Say: “That is outside this class's competency and risk scope. I can point you toward an appropriately qualified educator rather than improvising.”
- **A participant begins sharing details about someone else.** Say: “Please keep identifying details out of class discussion. Use a hypothetical version, or raise a confidential concern through the proper channel.”
- **A participant appears distressed.** Pause, offer choice and privacy, avoid requiring disclosure, and connect with appropriate staff or clinical/emergency support if needed.

## Classroom evaluation rubric

An effective participant can explain a relevant consent decision, identify a limit and a stop condition, describe an option that respects accessibility, and communicate when outside expertise is required. Educators should record what was confusing, not personal scene disclosures.

**Teacher self-review after class:** Were the learning outcomes met? Did anyone struggle with access or vocabulary? What activities ran long? Which safety clarifications were necessary? What sources require refreshing? Record revisions under a new version before repeating the class.
